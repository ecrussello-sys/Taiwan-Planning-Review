Post-Industrial Transformation

The Port That
Reinvented
Itself

How Kaohsiung turned abandoned warehouses and a declining harbor into one of Asia's most remarkable creative districts, and what Hamilton can learn from it.

Kaohsiung Music Center (2021) · Kaohsiung Harbor, Taiwan

One of the most remarkable urban transformations I have witnessed is Kaohsiung city. It has completely reinvented itself from a declining industrial port into a vibrant hub known for its art. As global shipping patterns shifted and the city's economy moved from industrial to service-oriented, the waterfront deteriorated, until a group of local artists rediscovered the abandoned Pier-2 warehouses during National Day fireworks celebrations in 2000 and established the Pier-2 Art Development Association in 2001 (Ministry of Culture, Taiwan, n.d.). What emerged was a creative district that now houses hundreds of shops, cafes, artist studios, and music venues, anchored further by the Kaohsiung Light Rail (2015) and the Kaohsiung Music Center (2021) (Kembel, 2025). Crucially, the city's approach was low-cost and incremental instead of a giant gentrification sweep that ignores the city's heritage. Improvements such as repurposing shipping containers into performance stages, concrete walls completely adorned by graffiti artists, and giant sculptures assembled from recycled steel, demonstrate that good public space infused with character and identity is possible without massive capital investment (Taiwan Tourism Administration, n.d.).

The Creative Class Framework

This trajectory aligns closely with Florida's (2002) The Rise of the Creative Class, which argues that investment into these desirable and dynamic environments attracts creative people, businesses, and capital to post-industrial cities.

To align with the "Creative Class" trajectory, Kaohsiung provides a textbook example of a post-industrial city pivoting toward a "lifestyle economy." By repurposing its heavy-industrial waterfront into the Asia New Bay Area, the city has moved beyond its identity as a mere port to become a hub for the creative and tech sectors.

Kaohsiung has mastered the adaptive reuse of its maritime assets to attract Florida's creative demographic. The Pier-2 Art Center now serves as the city's cultural anchor by filling these spaces with independent galleries, street art, and design studios, Kaohsiung has created the "authentic" aesthetic that attracts human capital. This shift demonstrates how physical environments can be re-engineered to facilitate the social and professional "spillovers" necessary for an innovation-led economy.

A Hamilton Parallel

As a near lifetime Hamiltonian, the parallel struck me instantly. Both cities were built on heavy industry (Hamilton on steel, Kaohsiung on fishing and shipping) and both suffered when those industries contracted. Hamilton rebranded around its natural geography, with the City of Waterfalls nonprofit cataloguing 130 waterfalls within city limits (Buist, 2015), as well as Supercrawl growing from 3,000 attendees in 2009 to over 285,000 by 2024 (Supercrawl, n.d.).

Both cities also illustrate that the strategic leveraging of water is not merely aesthetic but economically consequential. A study of Pittsburgh's Three Rivers waterfront found that every dollar of public investment in waterfront parkland generated roughly $20 in private development, with nearby property values rising 117% over fifteen years, far outpacing the city average (Reonomy, 2018).

The lesson from both Kaohsiung and Hamilton is clear: post-industrial cities should embrace their "rugged" cultural identity and reimagine otherwise overlooked and underused spaces to rewrite their narratives.

Hamilton's progress is undeniable, but it hasn't reached everyone equally. The city's industrial roots still cast a literal shadow over its air quality and while there is a shift toward "Green Steel," residents in the lower city still breathe in higher levels of particulate matter (PM2.5) and NO2 than those in purely residential towns. Additionally the housing crisis has become impossible to ignore. As of late 2025, over 1,300 people were identified as homeless (a 7% jump in just one year) leaving emergency shelters struggling to keep up with the nearly 2,000 neighbors currently without a permanent roof. This instability feeds into a heartbreaking opioid crisis: in 2025 alone, Hamilton lost 161 people to drug-related deaths, with paramedics responding to over 1,100 overdoses. It's a stark reminder that beneath the new glass condos and trendy cafes, many are still fighting a daily battle for health, safety, and a place to call home. These are factors that should be considered when reinventing our cities and should serve as a reminder of who cities are for.
