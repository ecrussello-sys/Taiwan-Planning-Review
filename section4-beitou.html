How Taipei transformed a reviled waste incinerator into a community landmark through interest-based negotiation, and what that means for planners everywhere.

Beitou Incineration Plant Sky Tower · Beitou District, Taipei

When Taipei opened the Beitou Incineration Plant in 1994, it was designed to process 1,200 tons of waste daily but was immediately met with NIMBY opposition. Residents feared the emissions and the degradation of Beitou's renowned hot springs landscape (Hsu and Chiu, 2010). Rather than treating this resistance as an obstacle to overcome, the Taipei City Government reframed it as a negotiating problem, one that required integrative solutions addressing community interests rather than simply imposing a decision.

Interest-Based Negotiation in Practice

Drawing on what Fisher and Ury (1981) describe as interest-based negotiation, the city moved beyond positional bargaining by first understanding what residents genuinely valued: health, amenity, and meaningful participation. The Taipei Government convened multiple public meetings to solicit feedback that was substantively incorporated into planning decisions (Taipei City Government, 2018). This participatory posture transformed residents from opponents into stakeholders, a foundational shift that opened space for collaborative problem-solving.

Furthermore, the city institutionalized accountability by making real-time emissions data publicly accessible, and the Environmental Protection Bureau issued regular, independent safety reports (Environmental Protection Administration, Taiwan, 2020).

Expanding the Pie: Community Value Over Concessions

Rather than simply offering light concessions to soften an unwanted outcome, the city "expanded the pie" by attaching genuine community value to the project. The proposed Sky Tower, with an observation deck and civic gathering space, reframed the plant as an attraction (Taipei City Government, 2018). Developing built-in gyms, parks, and a community center directly addressed residents' interest in quality of life, while careful landscaping and green buffers mitigated the plant's visual intrusion into the district's natural character (Lee, 2016).

These were strategic additions that changed the focus from minimizing resident burden to maximizing community benefit.
