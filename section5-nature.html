How Taiwan embeds Low Impact Development and Nature-Based Solutions into everyday infrastructure to fight urban heat and manage extreme rainfall.

Kaohsiung Light Rail on permeable grass-embedded tracks · Kaohsiung City

The integration of nature into the built environment relies on ecological tools known as Low Impact Development (LID) or Nature-Based Solutions (NBS), which are designed to mimic natural processes like infiltration and evapotranspiration (Meegle, 2026; WBDG, 2021). These tools, which include permeable pavements, green roofs, and urban forests, serve as "green infrastructure" to restore hydrological and thermal balances disrupted by urbanization (Encyclopedia.pub, n.d.). In the context of Taiwan, these strategies are critical for enhancing climate resilience, specifically by mitigating the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect and managing extreme seasonal rainfall (ResearchGate, 2026; Preprints.org, 2026).

Permeable Pavement: Cooling the City from the Ground Up

While large surface parking lots are uncommon in Taiwan (parking structures being far more prevalent), when open lots do exist, permeable parking spaces are a frequent sight. This is no coincidence. Taiwan's urban heat island intensity has been measured at 3.2 to 4.2°C above surrounding areas, with dense impervious surfaces identified as a primary driver (Lin et al., 2020). Permeable pavement directly counters this: a field study in Taipei found permeable surfaces ran 3.7°C cooler than impervious pavement and 4.5°C cooler than asphalt (Chen et al., 2023).

Permeable Paver Parking, Xinshi District, Tainan · Grass-jointed surface visible at night

The case for permeability is equally strong from a water management standpoint. Southern Taiwan sees roughly 90% of its annual rainfall in the wet season alone, making runoff reduction and groundwater recharge critical (Water Resources Agency, 2022). This is not only limited to parking lots. When visiting Kaohsiung, I noticed the Light Rail system also incorporated permeable grass-embedded sections into much of the line, with the only exceptions being where it likely proved structurally difficult, such as bridges.

Kaohsiung Light Rail · Grass-embedded permeable trackway, Kaohsiung City

Urban Greening: Trees as Infrastructure

Another way Taiwan utilizes nature to reduce heat burden is through urban greening. Research shows that tree canopy coverage of 40% or more at the city block scale can meaningfully reduce daytime air temperatures, a finding that Professor Michael Drescher argues must be treated as a matter of public priority given its implications for environmental justice (Drescher, 2019; Ziter et al., 2019). From personal observation, newer streets in Taiwan's developing areas are increasingly lined with trees on both sides, suggesting this awareness is being built directly into road design in a region where heat and humidity make canopy cover not just desirable, but essential.
