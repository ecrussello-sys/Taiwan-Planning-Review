Preface

Overview

This project is a short collection of different aspects of Taiwanese planning observed over the course of a four-month co-op, ranging from micromobility to social housing. It is designed to be easily digestible while offering adequate depth into each area of interest.

Taiwan, officially the Republic of China (ROC), is a densely populated island nation in East Asia with a population of approximately 23.2 million people (Ministry of the Interior, 2024). The demographics are predominantly Han Chinese, comprising roughly 95% of the population, as well as 16 officially recognized Indigenous Austronesian tribes, who represent about 2.5% of the population (Council of Indigenous Peoples, 2023).

Culturally, Taiwan serves as a unique mosaic of traditional Chinese values, Japanese colonial influences, and Western democratic ideals. This is reflected in a religious landscape dominated by Buddhism and Taoism, as well as a progressive social atmosphere that saw Taiwan become the first in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage (Foreign Ministry of the ROC, 2023).

Economically, the nation has transitioned from an agrarian society to a global technology powerhouse, driven largely by its dominance in the semiconductor industry through TSMC (Rigger, 2021).

Why Taiwan

Taiwan packs 23 million people into an island smaller than most of our Great Lakes. Every square metre of urban land must earn its keep. The planning systems that emerged from this constraint offer lessons far beyond the island's shores.

Scope of This Report

Sections cover everything from land value capture and infrastructure finance, zoning density and FAR trading, social housing delivery, micromobility integration, and nature-based urban planning.

Infrastructure Finance

The Land Value Capture Engine

The funding of municipal infrastructure in Taiwan is a balance between central government oversight and local ingenuity, heavily rooted in the concept of Land Value Capture. When a new MRT line is proposed, such as the expansion of the Circular Line in Taipei or the Yellow Line in Kaohsiung, the project is not funded by a simple tax pot.

Instead, the government utilizes the Forward-looking Infrastructure Development Program for massive capital injections from the central budget, combined with land readjustment schemes (Executive Yuan, 2023). In this process, the municipality takes over a block of underutilized land, installs new roads, utilities, and green spaces, and then returns a smaller but significantly more valuable portion to the original owners (Lin and Ding, 2024).

This "unearned increment" in land value is then used to pay for the infrastructure itself, effectively making the development self-financing (Yen et al., 2022). It is a model that transforms urban growth from a liability into a revenue stream.

How Land Readjustment Works

The municipality acquires underutilized land, installs roads, utilities, and parks, then returns a smaller but far more valuable parcel to original owners. The difference in value finances the infrastructure itself.

Forward-looking Program

The central government's Forward-looking Infrastructure Development Program provides large capital injections to anchor major transit expansions, working alongside local land readjustment to share fiscal risk across levels of government.

Zoning and Density

Floor Area as Currency

Zoning in major cities like Taipei or Taichung is defined by extreme density and a rigid adherence to Floor Area Ratio and Building Coverage Ratio, though these are often traded like a currency. In the heart of Taipei's Xinyi District, developers can gain significant floor area bonuses by incorporating public benefits such as green spaces.

With said bonuses, FARs can range from 200% to well over 800% (Ministry of the Interior, 2024). A specific example of this is the collaboration between the National Housing and Urban Regeneration Center (HURC) and private developers. By designating a portion of a new high-rise for social housing or public plazas, a developer can "buy" the right to build additional floors under the Urban Planning Act (MOI, 2024).

This creates a denser, more efficient city where commercial, residential, and social functions are stacked vertically, a stark contrast to the sprawling, single-use zoning patterns common in Canadian cities. The zoning map opposite illustrates how finely Taipei calibrates these land use categories, with parks, plazas, schools, infrastructure, and commercial zones woven together at a granular block-by-block scale.

Key Insight

By treating floor area ratio as a tradeable asset rather than a ceiling, Taiwan transforms density from a planning constraint into a tool for delivering public goods, without requiring direct government expenditure.
