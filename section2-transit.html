From a 13 NTD bike ticket in Taiwan to a GO Transit safety crisis, what a small surcharge taught me about infrastructure built around reality.

What GO Can Learn from Taiwan Local Trains

On one of my first days in Taiwan I set out to buy a bike and found one on Facebook Marketplace, three train stops away. After picking it up, I headed back to the station and was caught off guard when I was redirected to the ticketing office to purchase a separate ticket for the bike, and told to head to a dedicated car with bicycle "parking." While I was annoyed at shelling out an extra 13 NTD (about 50 cents), it made me think of an issue GO Transit has encountered back home.

An Emerging Crisis on GO Train

With rising unemployment and immigration, there has been an increase of couriers working for Uber Eats and DoorDash commuting into Toronto while living in outer-GTA municipalities where the cost of living is lower (BlogTO, 2024). Brampton became ground zero. Toronto is where the orders are, but no one can afford to live there anymore.

The trains weren't built for this. Photos showed GO train bike carriages packed wall-to-wall on the Kitchener line, leaving passengers no clear path through, "there is no way to evacuate if something happens." Fire risk compounded the problem: Toronto recorded 55 lithium-ion battery fires in 2023, nearly double the prior year, most involving e-bikes (CTV News, 2024).

Toronto vs. Taiwan, The Bike Car Problem

What Taiwan Quietly Figured Out

Taiwan, it turns out, had quietly figured something out. Designated bike cars have been a feature of Taiwan Railways since 2011, long before e-bikes became a flashpoint anywhere. A ticket for your bicycle, infrastructure built around reality. The system acknowledges that bikes belong on trains, it just asks that they do so on terms that keep everyone safe. What felt like a minor bureaucratic inconvenience to me was actually a small piece of thoughtful policy design decades in the making.
